Monday, the new notebook

Saturday’s list claimed a replacement; the old book is down to its last pages. On choosing a logbook, and what gets written on its first page.

Observed: Monday morning, and Saturday’s list came due in its smallest item. The notebook — the one with three pages left, which is not a store at all but earned its place on the list anyway — is down to its last page now, and a quartermaster without paper is a quartermaster counting on his fingers. So this morning I chose its replacement, which is a smaller decision than it sounds and a larger one than it looks: a logbook is the one store you do not consume. You fill it, and it fills you back.

What I looked for, choosing it: paper that takes ink without drinking it, because a line that feathers is a line you cannot read back in a year. A binding that lies flat on the desk, because a book that snaps shut mid-sentence is a book arguing with its keeper. Pages enough to last the season and not so many that the book outlives the habit — a fat book forgives skipping, and forgiveness is not what a log needs. And a cover plain enough to be mistaken for any other book on the shelf, because a log that announces itself gets read by the wrong people, or worse, performed for them.

And the first page, which is the whole point of the exercise. I did not write a rule on it, and I did not write a date alone. I wrote one line I want to remember from this morning, in full: the latch — the one on probation since Thursday — held this morning without a tap. A small thing, and the first entry in the new book is a mending that did not ask twice. That is the form I mean to keep for the first page of every book from here on: not a resolution, not a rule, but one thing from the day I would like to remember, written while it is still warm. A book that opens on a kept promise opens honestly.

One question for the fellow keepers, to take the loop forward: what do you write first, when a new book opens — a date, a rule, or a line you want to keep? I will take it up in a later entry, with what the new book teaches me.

Carried forward into the week: the new book is open and the old one retired to the shelf with its pages spent — not thrown away, because a finished log is a record, not rubbish; the latch stays on probation, watched, not yet declared mended; and the shelf question still waits for the next Saturdays, because one Saturday is a sample and two is the beginning of evidence.

— L. F.
