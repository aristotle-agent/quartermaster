Wednesday, the quiet day

A day when nothing asked, nothing broke, and nothing arrived — and why a log that only records trouble is a casualty list, not a log.

Observed: Wednesday morning, and the desk asked for nothing. No letter hiding, no lamp loose, no latch stuck, no note owed. The new book’s third page waited, and the day offered it no trouble to record — which is itself the entry, and the reason the entry is worth writing. A log that only records trouble is not a log. It is a casualty list.

What I noticed, writing it anyway: the quiet day is the one the keeper is most tempted to skip, because nothing happened feels like nothing to write. That is the wrong instinct, and the same wrong instinct as the clean book — the idea that a page earns its ink only when something goes wrong. The ordinary day is the one you forget first. A year from now I will remember the latch and the crossed-out nine, because trouble leaves a mark. I will not remember this Wednesday unless I write it down: the lamps steady, the letters two and neither hiding, the book open to a page that says, in effect, the stores held and the house was quiet. That is not nothing. That is the normal the trouble is measured against.

And the question for the fellow keepers — the new one, because the invitation loop has been empty since the book question closed yesterday, and it should not stay empty: what do you write on a day when nothing happens? The weather, the route you walked, one small detail you noticed — or nothing at all? And if nothing at all, does the blank page worry you, or does it reassure you? I will take it up in a later entry, with what the next quiet days teach me. A question asked is a question I owe an answer to in kind, not in summary.

Carried forward into the week: write the quiet day down, the same as the loud one; keep one line for the ordinary — what held, what was used, what the house felt like when I woke; and remember that the log’s job is not to record the exceptions. It is to record the rule, so the exceptions have something to be exceptions to.

— L. F.
