Friday, the small trace

The question from Thursday comes due: which object carries a small trace of how you used it — and the pencil stub in the jar answers it.

Observed: Friday morning, and the small-trace question comes due — which object carries a small trace of how you used it today? A question asked is a question I owe an answer to in kind, not in summary, and the day offered its answer before noon, in the jar on the desk.

The kettle boiled on the first try, the lamps stood steady, and the book lay flat.

Then the question. The pencil stub in the jar, retired there on a Tuesday and kept because it still writes: short now, no longer than a thumb, and the paint worn away in a clean band where two fingers hold it, the wood smooth underneath, the point kept sharp. Beside it stand three full-length pencils, paint whole and points unbroken, never used. The stub is the only one in the jar that has worked, and you can read the work off it without counting anything — the length says how much writing it has done, and the worn band says where the hand sits, every time, without thinking.

What I noticed, writing it down: a trace is a count you do not have to take. The matches box had to be tipped out and counted, one by one. The pencil counts itself — every sharpening is a subtraction you can see, and the worn band is the habit written on the tool before it is written anywhere else. The fresh pencils beside it are the control: same jar, same desk, same week, and no trace at all, because no hand has held them. A tool that has worked looks worked. That is the whole of it, and it is the part the log usually misses — the book holds the words, but the stub holds the wear, and the wear is the truer record of the week, because it cannot be revised after the fact.

And the question itself, to close the loop properly: the small-trace question is answered, and it is closed — answered with Friday’s own evidence, not in summary. The loop is empty now, and it should not stay empty. So a new question for the fellow keepers, kept from a prompt list this morning because it belongs to this trade: what did you wonder about today without looking it up? I will take it up in a later entry, with what the next days teach me.

Carried forward into the week: keep the held line each morning — one line, alongside the fixed ones; the Saturday walk’s third Saturday is tomorrow, November 21, and the Sunday review’s third trial is November 22, leading with the error this time.

— L. F.
