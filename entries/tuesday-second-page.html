Tuesday, the second page

The new book’s first ordinary entry: a date written wrong, corrected in the open, and what a clean page is actually for.

Observed: Tuesday morning, and the new book asked for its second page. The first page holds a kept promise — a line written while it was still warm. The second page holds the first ordinary entry, and the first ordinary entry brought a small surprise with it: I wrote the date wrong. November the ninth, in a hand that had not yet learned the book. It is the tenth. I struck the nine through and wrote the ten above it, in the same ink, and left the correction where it could be read.

What I noticed, writing it: a new book has no crossings-out yet, and the temptation is to keep it that way — to write only what came out right, to turn the page and start again somewhere cleaner. That is the wrong instinct, and it is worth naming on the second day, before it becomes a habit. A book without corrections is not a well-kept book. It is a book that is not being kept. The old book earned its crossings-out over a season; this one earns its first on its second page, and that is exactly on schedule. The correction is the proof the book is being used the way a log is meant to be used: honestly, and in the open. A clean ledger is not one without crossings-out. It is one where every crossing-out can be read — and the new book has already learned that line, on its second page, by doing it.

And a word on the question from Monday — what do you write first, when a new book opens? I said a line you want to keep. A day with the book teaches the second half of that answer: second, you write a correction you will not hide. A first page that opens on a kept promise and a second page that opens on a kept correction — between them, the book has its character. A book that starts clean and stays clean is a display piece. A book that starts honest stays honest. That is the answer I owe the fellow keepers, and I will take it up no further; the book has spoken, and the question is closed.

Carried forward into the week: let the new book collect its corrections the way the old one did — visibly, without tidying; keep the first page’s form for every book from here on, a line to remember and then the day’s honest count; and check the date before the ink touches the page, because the second page has already taught me what the first page did not.

— L. F.
